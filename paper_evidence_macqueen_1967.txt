--- Section 1: Introduction (Page 1) ---
"SOME METHODS FOR CLASSIFICATION AND ANALYSIS OF MULTIVARIATE OBSERVATIONS"
Author: J. MACQUEEN, UNIVERSITY OF CALIFORNIA, LOS ANGELES

"The main purpose of this paper is to describe a process for partitioning an N-dimensional population into k sets on the basis of a sample. The process, which is called 'k-means,' appears to give partitions which are reasonably efficient in the sense of within-class variance. That is, if p is the probability mass function for the population, S={S1,S2,...,Sk} is a partition of EN, and ui, i=1,2,...,k, is the conditional mean of p over the set Si, then W^2(S)=integral over Si of |z-ui|^2 dp(z) tends to be low for the partitions S generated by the method."

--- Section 2.1: The k-means Process (Pages 3) ---
The core algorithm definition (verbatim from formula 2.1-2.2):

"Relative to a given k-tuple X=(X1,X2,...,Xk), xi in EN, i=1,2,...,k, we define a minimum distance partition S(x)={S1(x),S2(x),...,Sk(x)} of EN, by:
(2.1) Si(x)=Ti(x)\S2(x)\S1(x)... \Sk-1(x),
where
(2.2) Ti(x)={z:z in EN, |z-xi| < |z-xj|, j=1,2,...,k}.
The set Si(x) contains the points in EN nearest to xi, with tied points being assigned arbitrarily to the set of lower index."

"Sample k-means x^(n) = Z^(n)(Z1^(n), Z2^(n), ..., Zk^(n)), zi^(n) in EN, i=1,2,...,k, with associated integer weights Wi^(n) are now defined as follows:
x_i^1 = z_i, W_i^1 = 1, i=1,2,...,k,
and for n=1,2,..., if z_{k+n} in S_j(x^(n)) then:
x_j^{n+1} = (W_j^n * x_j^n + z_{k+n}) / (W_j^n + 1),
W_j^{n+1} = W_j^n + 1,
and x_j^{n+1} = x_j^n, W_j^{n+1} = W_j^n for j ≠ i,
where S_n = {S_n^1, S_n^2, ..., S_n^k} is the minimum distance partition relative to x^(n)."

"Stated in informally, the k-means procedure consists of simply starting with k groups each of which consists of a single random point, and thereafter adding each new point to the group whose mean the new point is nearest. After a point is added to a group, the mean of that group is adjusted in order to take account of the new point. Thus at each stage the k-means are, in fact, the means of the groups they represent (hence the term k-means)."

--- Theoretical Framework (Page 3, formulas 2.3-2.5) ---
"W(X) = integral over E of sum_i |z - x_i|^2 dp(z),
V(X) = integral over E of sum_i min_j |z - x_j|^2 dp(z),
and V(X) <= W(X)"

"xi is unbiased relative to pi if xi = u_i where u_i = integral over Si(x) of z dp(z)/pi"

--- Section 2.2: Proofs (Pages 4-8) ---
Contains convergence proofs using martingale theory. Lemma 3 states conditions for almost sure convergence of stochastic processes related to the k-means updates. Theorem 1 establishes almost sure convergence of the k-means under certain conditions.

Key formula (2.8):
"E[W(x^{n+1}) | W_n] <= W(x^n) - sum_i E[|x_i^n - u_i^n|^2 * (p_i^n)^2 * (2W_i^n + 1)/(W_i^n + 1)^2] + ... "

--- Section: Applications (Page 9) ---
"A computer program actually prepared for this purpose involved several modifications of the k-means process... First, the program involves two parameters: C for 'coarsening,' and R for 'refinement.'"

"If the distance between them is less than C, they are averaged together, using their respective weights, to form a single mean. The nearest pair is again determined, their separation compared with C..."

"For refinement, the program compares the within-group variation with the between-group variation. If the ratio exceeds R, the largest group is split into two by dividing it along the direction of greatest spread..."

--- Section: Computer Experiment (Pages 9-12) ---
Example 1: "The data were obtained from the Semantic Differential study carried out at Swarthmore College... Selecting at random a half-dozen words from several groups obtained from these semantic differential data..."

Example 2 (Pages 11-12): "A sample of 250 four-dimensional random vectors was prepared, with the values on each dimension being independently and uniformly distributed on the integers 1 through 10. Two of the dimensions were then arbitrarily selected, and if with respect to the two dimensions a point was either 'high' (above 5) on both or 'low' (5 or less) on both, it was called an A; otherwise, it was called a B. This gave 121 A's and 129 B's... The k-means with k=8 were then obtained for the A's and B's separately."
